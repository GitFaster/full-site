# Success Principles: A Strategy Script for ShameGrin

*Drawn only from public primary material: Jeff Bezos's Amazon shareholder letters (1997 and 2016), Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters (1985, 1996) and "An Owner's Manual", Larry Page and Sergey Brin's 2004 Google IPO letter, Mark Zuckerberg's 2012 Facebook IPO letter, and Elon Musk's 2013 TED interview. Written for Gary, an independent musician (ShameGrin) who also builds his own iOS apps.*

**How to read this:** every principle shows who it comes from, a short quote I checked against the source text (or a clearly labeled paraphrase), and one concrete thing to do as a solo creator. There are no invented quotes, numbers or sources. The sources are listed at the end.

## The principles

### 1. Obsess over the customer, not the competition

**What it means:** Start from the person you serve and work backward. Customers are never fully satisfied, and that dissatisfaction is where you find the next thing worth making. Don't decide by watching rivals or by guessing from averages. Study real people and individual stories.

**Who:** Jeff Bezos (1997 and 2016 letters). Larry Page and Sergey Brin (2004 IPO letter: "Serving our end users is at the heart of what we do and remains our number one priority").

> "customers are always beautifully, wonderfully dissatisfied, even when they report being happy and business is great." (Bezos, 2016 letter to shareholders)

Bezos also warns against letting surveys stand in for real understanding: good inventors "study and understand many anecdotes rather than only the averages you'll find on surveys."

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Every week, read every App Store review, support email, and comment under your tracks, and copy the five most specific ones (complaints, requests, "I wish it…") into one running file. Choose your next app feature or next release only from that file, never from what another app or artist just shipped.

### 2. Play long games

**What it means:** Judge decisions by where they leave you in five to ten years, not by this week's numbers. A long horizon lets you make investments that short-term players won't, and it lets compounding do the heavy lifting.

**Who:** Jeff Bezos (1997 letter, section "It's All About the Long Term"), Larry Page and Sergey Brin (2004 IPO letter, "Long term focus"), Warren Buffett (1996 letter).

> "We will continue to make investment decisions in light of long-term market leadership considerations rather than short-term profitability considerations or short-term Wall Street reactions." (Bezos, 1997 letter)

> "A management team distracted by a series of short term targets is as pointless as a dieter stepping on a scale every half hour." (Page and Brin, 2004 IPO letter)

> "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes." (Buffett, 1996 letter)

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Write a one-page "2031 picture": the catalog size, the number of apps, and the audience you want five years from now. Before you take on any project, deal or release plan, ask whether it moves you toward that picture or only feels good this month. Check your streams and downloads once a week, not every day.

### 3. Reason from first principles, not by analogy

**What it means:** Most of the time we copy what others do with small changes, and that's fine for everyday life. But when you want to do something new, break the problem down to the facts you know are true (costs, physics, what people actually need) and rebuild the solution from there.

**Who:** Elon Musk (TED interview with Chris Anderson, February 2013).

> "boil things down to their fundamental truths and reason up from there, as opposed to reasoning by analogy." (Musk, TED 2013)

In the same answer he describes reasoning by analogy as "copying what other people do with slight variations."

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Take one assumption you've inherited, such as "a release needs a label-style rollout," "an app needs a subscription," or "a video needs a big budget." Write down its real parts: what it costs, what the listener or user actually gets, and what your tools can do today. Then design the cheapest version that delivers that core value. Do this for one assumption every month.

### 4. Ship fast, then iterate

**What it means:** Speed compounds. Release small, learn from what real people do, and improve. Most decisions can be undone, so make them quickly on incomplete information and get good at correcting course. Waiting for perfect usually means waiting forever.

**Who:** Mark Zuckerberg (2012 IPO letter, "The Hacker Way"), Jeff Bezos (2016 letter, "High-Velocity Decision Making").

> "We have the words 'Done is better than perfect' painted on our walls to remind ourselves to always keep shipping." (Zuckerberg, 2012 letter)

> "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had. If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow." (Bezos, 2016 letter)

Bezos calls reversible decisions "two-way doors" that deserve "a light-weight process." Zuckerberg quotes the hacker mantra "Code wins arguments": build a prototype instead of debating.

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Set a fixed shipping rhythm, for example one TestFlight build and one finished song or snippet every two weeks, released at "good enough." Before each choice, ask "two-way door or one-way door?" For two-way doors (cover art, pricing tests, a feature flag), decide the same day.

### 5. Make bold bets, but make sure no single bet can sink you

**What it means:** Big outcomes come from a portfolio of swings, some of which will fail. So take real risks, but size each one so a failure teaches you something instead of ending you. Think about what would ruin you, and stay away from it.

**Who:** Jeff Bezos (1997 letter), Larry Page and Sergey Brin (2004 IPO letter), Mark Zuckerberg (2012 letter), Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger (1996 letter).

> "We will make bold rather than timid investment decisions where we see a sufficient probability of gaining market leadership advantages. Some of these investments will pay off, others will not, and we will have learned another valuable lesson in either case." (Bezos, 1997 letter)

> "The riskiest thing is to take no risks." (a saying Zuckerberg quotes in the 2012 letter, under "Be Bold")

The other side, from Buffett's 1996 letter quoting Munger: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there." Buffett adds: "If we can't tolerate a possible consequence, remote though it may be, we steer clear of planting its seeds." Page and Brin describe funding projects with "a 10% chance of earning a billion dollars," as smaller side bets next to the core business.

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Keep roughly 80% of your time on the core (your catalog and your main app) and 20% on one deliberate long shot per quarter, such as an experimental genre, a new app idea, or a collaboration. Write down your "ruin list" (debt you can't service, burning out, losing your masters or app accounts) and never take a bet that touches it.

### 6. Do the things that do not scale

**What it means:** Early on, the work that grows a project is personal and slow: finding listeners or users one by one, and making the first ones glad they showed up. A big public launch is a poor substitute for that.

**Who:** Paul Graham, "Do Things that Don't Scale" (2013). This is a paraphrase of the essay, not a quotation. Source: https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Each week, personally send the new song or the new TestFlight build to ten specific people and ask one question. Keep a list of what they actually did with it.

### 7. Stay with work you can love, and keep an appetite

**What it means:** The 2005 Stanford commencement address, as published by Stanford, closes on staying hungry and staying foolish, after talking about loving the work and about death as a way to cut through other people's expectations.

**Who:** Steve Jobs, Stanford commencement address, 2005. Source: https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2005/06/youve-got-find-love-jobs-says

> "Stay hungry. Stay foolish." (Jobs, closing line of that address, as carried in the Stanford publication of the speech)

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Once a year, drop one project you are only finishing because you already started it. Put that time into the song or the app you would still make if nobody were watching.

### 8. Compound your own work

**What it means:** Sam Altman's essay "How To Be Successful" argues for letting your own output compound, for focus, and for being pulled by your own standards rather than by status. This sentence is a paraphrase. The essay is the source, and no line from it is quoted here.

**Who:** Sam Altman. Source: https://blog.samaltman.com/how-to-be-successful

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Pick one catalog and one app as the things that compound. Say no to a feature or a remix that does not make that catalog or that app more useful next year.

### 9. Keep your own money in it, and say when it is failing

**What it means:** Buffett's "An Owner's Manual" tells Berkshire owners that the managers keep their wealth in the business and that the reports should be candid. The 1985 letter describes shutting the textile operation instead of feeding a business that could not earn its keep. Both of those are paraphrases of those documents.

**Who:** Warren Buffett, An Owner's Manual, and the 1985 letter. Sources: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/ownman.pdf and https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1985.html

**How to apply it (ShameGrin):** Keep the masters and the app accounts in your own name. Once a quarter, write down the release or the feature that is not earning its time, and stop it.
